The unmetered day

Most of what gets called hard work is long work, and the two are almost unrelated.

I know this because I have done a great deal of the long kind. On the trading floors I worked on, the day is 13 hours, and the day is not a metaphor. You arrive before the exchange opens, you leave after it closes and the post-trade jobs have run, and the hours in between are sitting and coding and trying one more thing. Nobody asked me to do this. It was the shape of the room, and I fitted the room.

For a long time I believed those hours were the work. If a week had 65 of them it had been a good week. This is the first thing I want to take apart, because I think a lot of people who consider themselves hard workers are actually counting the same wrong thing I was.

Here is the wrong thing. Hours are the only quantity that is always available to be measured. Progress on a hard problem is not visible day to day, quality is not visible until someone else reads the code, and whether the thing you built mattered will not be known for a year. Hours are visible at 9pm when you look at the clock. So the ambitious person, who badly wants a number to be going up, picks the one number that can be read off the wall. I picked it. It felt like rigour and it was closer to superstition.

Compare a different kind of day. In my first year at college I joined the startup club and tried everything, and nothing worked, and the reason nothing worked was not effort. It was that I had no skills yet. You cannot outwork the absence of a skill. The following year I did much less and got much further, because the less was aimed at one thing, which was becoming a person who could actually build the things I kept trying to build. Those months had no meter on them. Nobody saw them. There was no clock on the wall reading 13.

That is the distinction I would draw now. Long work is measured by the meter. Hard work is what happens when the meter is off.

Hard work has three properties that long work does not. It is directed at the specific thing you are worst at, which is unpleasant, which is why people prefer long work. It has a test at the end that you did not write yourself. And it is mostly boring. Richard Hamming put the arithmetic on this in a talk at Bell Labs in 1986: given two people of roughly the same ability, if one works ten percent more than the other, the second will more than twice outproduce the first, because knowledge compounds like interest. People quote that line as a case for long hours. Read the rest of the talk and it is the opposite. Hamming's ten percent is spent on the important problem while everyone else spends theirs on the urgent one. It is a claim about direction, not duration.

I will give you the version of this I trust most, because it is the one that cost me money. This summer we decided to find out how badly language models actually do on C++, rather than repeat what people were saying about it. There were 109 public benchmarks that touched the question. We reproduced them. Not read them, ran them, one at a time, checked the harness, checked the scoring, found where the numbers had been quietly inflated. It took weeks and there is no visible output from most of those days at all, because the output of reproducing a benchmark correctly is a number that matches the paper, which is nothing. That is what the unmetered day looks like. From the outside it is indistinguishable from doing nothing. From the inside it is the only reason I can now say what is true.

Here is the part I would rather not write.

The 13-hour day was partly a way of not deciding. If you fill every hour, you never have to ask which hour mattered, and you never have to face the possibility that the answer is two of them. A long day is an anaesthetic for that question. I did not know this while I was inside it. I found out when I left, and the first weeks on my own were terrifying in a way the floor never was, because nobody was setting the hours and I had to say, at 10am, what the work was. It turned out I had spent years being disciplined about showing up and almost no time being disciplined about choosing. Those are different muscles and I had only trained one.

So the useful thing I can give you is a test. At the end of a day, do not ask how long it was. Ask what you can now do that you could not do this morning, or what you now know that you did not know. If the answer is nothing, and the day was 13 hours, you have done long work. Some weeks that is the correct thing to do, because there are jobs that are just volume. But do not confuse it with the other thing, and do not let the meter tell you that you are working hard when you are working long.

Since July we have shipped a new version of what we are building every two or three days. It looks like the long kind of work, and there are plenty of long hours in it. But the clock is no longer what I watch. What I watch is whether each version fixes a thing a user hit that the last version did not. Some days that takes 14 hours. Some days it takes two and the rest of the day is me trying to look busy to myself.

The meter is still on the wall. I have just stopped reading it as a score. Anyone who has done both kinds of day knows which one they are actually afraid of.

Good enough is a decision somebody makes

Every piece of work has a point where somebody says it is done, and that person is never the work.

We talk about standards as if they were properties of things. A high-quality codebase, a rigorous paper, a clean result. But a codebase does not have a standard. Someone looked at it and decided to stop. The standard is the stopping rule, and the stopping rule lives in a person. Which means the entire question of quality reduces to a social one: who is allowed to say not yet, and will they say it when it costs them.

Steve Jobs used to tell a story about his father, who built fences and cabinets, and who insisted on finishing the back of the fence and the inside of the drawer as carefully as the front. Nobody would see the back. The father's answer was that he would know. People quote this as a story about craftsmanship. I read it as a story about who the inspector is. In the absence of a customer who will check, the father appointed himself, and held the appointment.

Here is the thing I noticed only recently. In most of my life the inspector was supplied. Exams have a grader. Trading has a P&L. Compilers reject. I did not have to hold the standard because something outside me held it and I merely had to meet it. I assumed this meant I had high standards. What it meant was that I lived among strict inspectors. Take them away and you find out what you actually have.

I found out when I chose who to build for. The people who write C and C++ for a living are, as a group, the most sceptical programmers alive. They have spent careers watching tools promise things and then segfault, so their default answer to anything new is no, and their second answer is to tell you exactly why in public. When we started building for them we were scolded, a lot, and early. It would have been far more pleasant to build for a crowd that says nice work. I stayed with the sceptics on purpose, because a not yet from someone who does not want to give you a yes is the only kind that tells you anything.

That is the first practical thing I would offer. You do not raise your standards by resolving to. You raise them by choosing whose not yet you have to hear. Most people do the opposite. They pick an audience that will say it looks fine, because it is pleasant, and then they wonder why the work is fine.

The second practical thing is about the other direction, being the inspector for someone else, and this is where it costs.

Last year I spent two hours with an engineer trying to get him to try a tool. He writes C++ for a living, he had decided the tool was garbage on principle, and my case was only that he should run it once and judge from the output. Two hours. I could have stopped at ten minutes with "fair enough" and we would both have felt respected. I did not stop, because I was sure, and because a not yet that you abandon when it gets awkward is not a standard, it is a preference. He ran it. His verdict afterwards was one word, and it was not garbage.

I mention the two hours because that is the actual price of a standard. It is not the price of doing the work carefully. It is the price of the conversation in which you tell another person the thing is not done, and they do not want to hear it, and you stay in the room. Almost everyone who says they have high standards means they hold them for themselves in private. Very few hold them out loud for someone who can push back.

There is a third role, and it is the one nobody trains for. Being the person told not yet. A standard only survives between two people if the one hearing it can take a hard sentence as care rather than as attack, and the one saying it can say it without anger. Most teams fail on one side or the other. Either the not yet is delivered as contempt and the relationship pays for it, or it is received as contempt and never said again. Kind words are not the same as care, and harsh words are not the same as hatred. The teams that build things worth building are the ones where both people know that, and can criticise each other on Monday and eat together on Monday night.

Now the part where I am the one being inspected.

We keep a document in the company that lists every claim we are permitted to make in public, with the evidence for each, and a second list of claims we are banned from making because we cannot prove them. The banned list exists because of me. Under pressure, in a meeting, with a good story running, I have said the strong version of a thing when only the weak version was true. Not lies, exactly. Numbers I had heard once and repeated as if I had measured them. The kind of rounding-up that everyone in a room full of founders does, and that a room full of engineers would catch in one click. I caught myself doing it, more than once, and I did not trust myself to stop by resolving to. So we wrote it down and made the list the inspector. A claim is either on the permitted list with a source, or it does not leave my mouth.

I want to be clear about what that admits. It admits that my standard for my own words, unsupervised, was lower than my standard for other people's code. That is an uncomfortable asymmetry to notice about yourself, and I think it is the common one. We are all strict about the things we are good at and lenient about the things we are merely doing.

So here is the test I use now. For any piece of work, ask three questions. Who will say not yet, by name. What will it cost them to say it. And when they say it, will I stay in the room. If the answer to the first question is nobody, the work has no standard, whatever it looks like. If the answer is somebody who will pay nothing for saying it, the standard is decorative. The standard is real only when the not yet is expensive and gets said anyway.

Jobs's father had no customer for the back of the fence. He made one up and obeyed him for forty years. The fences are gone. The habit went into the son, and from the son into objects that more than a billion people have now held, every one of which has a back that was finished as carefully as the front. That is what a standard actually is. Not a property of the fence. A person who would not stop, and another person watching, who learned.

Nobody remembers your great-great-grandfather

Name one thing your great-great-grandfather did. Not his name, which you may know. A thing he did, a fight he had, something he built or lost or was proud of.

Almost nobody can. Four generations is roughly a hundred years, and a hundred years is enough to erase a life completely, including from the memory of the people who exist only because it happened. His hardships are gone. His enemies are gone, and so is whatever they fought about. If he did something extraordinary, you do not know what it was. If he did nothing, the result is identical.

That is the forward half of the argument, and it is the half everyone knows. In a hundred years everyone you are trying to impress will be dead, everyone you envy will be dead, everyone you cannot stand will be dead, and nobody will remember any of it. People usually take this as a reason to relax. Nothing lasts, so why strain.

I take it the other way, and I want to explain why, because I think the relaxed reading is a mistake in arithmetic.

Here is the backward half, which almost nobody says. You exist because an unbroken chain of people survived long enough to have children, in conditions most of us would not last a winter in. Famines, wars, plagues, migrations on foot. Thousands of them, every one of whom made it to at least one child, or you are not here. Not one of them is remembered. Not one of them did it so that a descendant could give a moderate effort and go home on time.

Put the two halves together and the conclusion inverts. Forwards, nothing you do will be remembered, so there is no reputation to protect and no audience to perform for. Backwards, an enormous amount was paid for you to be standing here at all. The only thing that is actually disrespectful, in that light, is to be average on purpose. Not to fail. Failing is fine, most of the chain failed at most things. To coast. To be the link that had every advantage the chain had bought and decided to take it easy.

Seneca said the relaxed version was the mistake two thousand years ago. Life is long enough, he wrote, and a sufficiently generous amount has been given to us for the highest achievements if it were all well invested. His complaint was never that life is short. It was that people live as if the account were infinite, spend it on nothing they could name, and are surprised at the end. We are not given a short life but we make it short.

So this is how I actually use the hundred-year idea, and it is not for peace of mind. I use it to remove excuses. When I catch myself deciding that something is good enough, or that a thing can wait until tomorrow, or that a person's opinion of me matters, I run the great-great-grandfather test. Will any of this exist in a hundred years? No. Then the opinion does not matter and I can stop managing it. But the effort is a different question, because the effort is the only part of this that was ever mine to decide, and it is the one thing the whole chain behind me had in common.

If nothing is going to matter, the correct response is not to do less. It is to do extraordinary things, because there is no longer any reason not to. The fear of looking foolish is gone, since nobody will remember the foolishness. The fear of failing publicly is gone, for the same reason. What remains is the work itself and whether you gave it everything, and that is the one thing you will know, in the only window in which knowing is possible, which is now.

Now the part I would rather leave out.

I did not learn this on a mountain. I learned it at three in the morning, writing a long, angry letter to someone I work with, about urgency and effort and standards, the kind of letter you write when you have four hours before the next call and cannot sleep. It was not a calm document. Somewhere in the last third I found myself writing the great-great-grandfather question, and the chain, and the line about nothing mattering in two hundred years, and I noticed as I wrote it that every sentence in the letter applied to me first. The person I was really furious with was the version of myself that had ever, on any day, given eighty percent and called it a day.

That is the honest origin of the idea in my life. It is not serenity about death. It is fury at waste, mine included, with mortality as the argument that finally made the fury make sense.

So I would offer this, to anyone who has ever heard "nothing matters in the long run" and felt it pull them toward the couch. You have read it backwards. Nothing lasting means nothing is at risk. No audience means no stage fright. No memory means no permanent record of the failures. Everything that usually stops a person from attempting something large has just been removed, and the only cost left is effort, which was always the cheapest thing you had.

Your great-great-grandfather is gone, along with everything he did. So is his father, and his. The chain does not remember its links. It only continues through the ones who gave it everything they had.

Ambition is a recruiting problem

Nobody with a large ambition can execute it alone, which means every ambition is, underneath, a list of people you have not yet persuaded.

I want to start there because the usual conversation about ambition is about the size of the want. How big is your vision, how much do you want it, are you thinking big enough. Paul Graham's essay on great work says ambitious people are, if anything, already too conservative about it. I agree with him and I think the framing still points the wrong way, because it treats ambition as a quantity inside one person. In practice it is a headcount. The moment your ambition exceeds what one person can do in a lifetime, and any ambition worth the name does, the entire problem becomes who else is going to do it with you, and why they would.

Here is the first time I understood this, though I did not have the words then.

In my second year at college my grades were bad, a seven out of ten, and I wanted to do a project that was beyond me. There was a classmate who had scored a perfect ten every semester. I did not try to become him. I went to him with a trade. He would build the thing correctly, because he could and I could not, and I would make the professor understand that it was usable, because I could and he could not. Everyone asked why those two were working together. What they were actually asking was why a ten would let a seven onto his roster. The answer is that I had found the one thing he needed and could not supply himself, and offered it plainly.

That trade is the whole mechanism. Ambition recruits by finding the thing a better person lacks and being that thing. It does not recruit by being impressive. The people you most need are precisely the ones who are not impressed by you, because they have their own list.

When I am asked now what I look for in a place to work, the answer has been the same for years: an interesting problem and a high-calibre team, in that order, and the order is slightly dishonest because the second one is the one I actually check first. A hard problem with weak people is a hard problem you will not solve. A modest problem with strong people becomes a harder problem within a month, because strong people are bored by modest problems and enlarge them. The team is the ambition. The problem is just where you point it.

Money is the part everyone gets wrong here, so let me put it in one sentence. Money is the cheapest resource you can acquire, and the people who are good enough to matter already know that. You cannot recruit them with it. You can pay them with it, which is different. What recruits them is the roster, meaning who else has already said yes, and the trade, meaning the specific thing they get to do here that they cannot do where they are. I have watched many pitches, some of them mine, that led with the size of the market and the money on the table, to people who nodded politely and went back to a job they were not thrilled with. The size of the want does not move them. The name of the person who joined last week does.

Now for what it costs, because this is where the essay has to earn something.

Ambition is expensive for the people around it and the expense is asymmetric. The person with the ambition is compensated in the currency they care about, which is the attempt itself. Everyone they recruit is paid in a currency they may not have chosen. A co-founder gives up a salary. A friend gives up evenings for a year. A family gives up a person who is present. And every one of those people agreed to the plan before it worked, on the strength of a story, told by someone who was, at the time, mostly guessing.

I treat people's time as a resource to be sized. I know this about myself. When I am building a roster I think in terms of what each person can contribute and what it will cost to get them, and I do the arithmetic in my head while they are still talking. It is a useful habit and it is not a kind one. The people who have joined me on things did not experience themselves as a line in a plan. They experienced a friend asking. The gap between how I was thinking and how they were hearing is the actual cost of my ambition, and it is paid by them, not by me.

The honest version of this is that wanting a great deal makes you hard to be around. Not because ambitious people are unpleasant, though some are, but because a large want distorts every relationship it touches into a question of usefulness. You start to see people as either on the roster or not. The ones not on it get less of you. The ones on it get a version of you that is always, quietly, evaluating. I do not think this can be entirely fixed. I think it can be seen, and named, and paid for on purpose rather than by accident.

So here is what I would offer anyone who recognises the want in themselves.

First, stop measuring your ambition by its size and start measuring it by its roster. Write down the five people you would need and do not yet have. That list is your ambition, in its real form. If you cannot name them, the want is still a feeling rather than a plan.

Second, for each one, name the trade. Not what you can pay them. What they cannot get where they are that they could get with you. If there is no answer, you are not ready to ask.

Third, count the cost on their side of the ledger, not yours, and say it to them before they join. People will pay an extraordinary amount for a real attempt at something large. They will not forgive being told afterwards that they were a resource.

The ten-pointer and I finished the project. He went on to a firm where the bar is as high as any in the world, and I went on to do things that needed a great many other people to say yes. I still think about that trade, because it was the first time I understood that the want was never the hard part. The want I had at nineteen and the want I have now are the same size. What changed is the number of people who have agreed to carry it, and the honesty with which I asked them.

Staying

Everything ever written against comfort was written by someone who had already left it, and you should discount the argument accordingly.

I say this as one of those people. I walked out of a well-paid seat in a good firm and I have spent a fair amount of the time since telling friends who are still in similar seats that they should do the same. Some of them have listened. I am not sure I was right, and I am fairly sure my reasons for saying it were not the reasons I gave.

So let me try to write the honest version, which has to start by taking comfort seriously as a thing a reasonable person might want.

A comfortable job is a thermostat. It holds the temperature. You arrive, the problems are the size you can handle, the pay arrives on the date it says, the people are the people from last year, and the future is a version of the present with slightly larger numbers. Nothing about this is contemptible. Most of human history was spent trying to build exactly this room and failing. Someone who has it and chooses to keep it is not asleep. They have looked at the alternatives, which are colder and less predictable, and preferred the room. That is a decision, and it deserves to be argued with as one, not sneered at.

The sneering is what people who have left do, and I want to explain where it comes from, because I have felt it from the inside.

When you leave a comfortable equilibrium you take on a debt. The debt is the possibility that you were wrong, and the only way to service it is to keep believing the room was worse than it was. So you tell the story of the room in a particular way. It was stagnant. Nobody there was growing. They did not even know what was happening outside. Some of this is true. Some of it is what you need to be true to sleep. And the person still in the room becomes, without meaning to, a threat to your story, because if they are fine then perhaps you did not have to go. So you tell them to leave. Not for them. For you.

I have done this. I have sat with former colleagues who are content, well paid, doing interesting enough work, and I have made the case that they are being left behind by a world they cannot see. The case is not false. But when I listen back to myself making it, what I hear is not concern. I hear a person defending a decision.

Here, then, is the argument I think actually holds, once you strip out the self-defence.

Comfort is not the problem. Not looking is the problem, and the two are usually confused because they tend to arrive together. The thermostat does not just hold the temperature inside the room. It removes your reason to check the temperature outside. Nothing in a comfortable job rewards you for knowing what is happening beyond it, and quite a lot punishes you for it, because knowing tends to make you restless and restlessness is inconvenient for everyone. So the person in the room stops looking. Not because they are lazy, but because looking has no payoff in the room and a real cost.

And this is the one thing I would actually say to someone who is staying. Stay, if the room is what you want. But keep a window. Know, specifically and currently, what is happening in your field outside the building. Know what the tools are, what people are paid elsewhere, what a version of you who left two years ago is doing now. Not so that you leave. So that staying remains a decision rather than a default. The test is simple. Can you describe, in detail, the life you are choosing not to have? If yes, you are staying. If no, you are not staying, you are just still there, and those are different conditions even though they look identical from the corridor.

The people I worry about are not the ones who chose the room. They are the ones who cannot say what is outside it. I have talked to engineers, good ones, who did not know that the tools they would be using in two years already existed, and were being used daily by people a few miles away. They were not unhappy. That is the point. Unhappiness would have made them look. Comfort had removed the one signal that might have.

Now the admission, since the essay has been circling it.

I left the room partly because I could not stop looking, and looking made the room intolerable in a way it had not been the year before. Nobody should mistake that for courage. It is closer to a temperament, and I am not sure it is a good one. The people who can hold the thermostat steady, do fine work inside it for twenty years, and go home to a life that is not the job, have something I do not have and probably cannot acquire. When I tell them to leave I am, in part, asking them to become like me so that I feel less alone in the cold. That is not advice. That is company-seeking.

So I have stopped giving the advice, mostly. What I do instead is describe the outside as accurately as I can, with numbers where I have them, and then stop. If the description makes someone restless, the restlessness is theirs and they will do something with it. If it does not, they have chosen the room with their eyes open, and that is the only version of staying I have no argument against.

I still think most rooms are colder than they feel from inside. I still think the thermostat is lying about the weather. But I have noticed that I think this most loudly on the days when my own decision is going badly, and that has taught me to hold the opinion more quietly. The person who stays and knows exactly what they are giving up has made a harder call than I did. I only had to leave. They have to keep choosing, every year, with the window open.

Two nodes and a thin wire

Two people working on the same hard thing are two computers joined by a very thin wire, and almost every failure between them is a failure of the wire, not of the computers.

I started thinking about it this way when my co-founder and I ended up on opposite sides of the planet for a few months. Each of us had a full day of information the other did not: who we had met, what they had said, what had broken, what we now believed. Each of us also had a great deal of compute. What we did not have was bandwidth. A handful of messages a day, a call when the time zones allowed, and a shared channel that was always slightly behind. The wire was the constraint. Everything else was fine.

Once you see it as a wire, the rules for using it stop being manners and become engineering.

Claude Shannon showed in 1948 that every channel has a capacity, a hard ceiling on how much information can cross it per unit time, and that the way to approach the ceiling is to encode well, meaning to strip what the receiver already knows and send only what they do not. That is the whole of good communication between two people who work together, and most of us do the opposite. We send raw. We send the story in the order it happened to us, with the conclusion at the end where it arrived in our own heads, and the receiver has to decode the entire stream to find the one bit that changes what they do.

So the first rule is to compress before you send. Process the information fully on your own node first. What is the conclusion. What does the other person need to do differently because of it. What are the two or three reasons, in order of weight. Then send that, conclusion first, reasons after, in the fewest words that survive being misread. Consultants call this the pyramid principle. Engineers would call it not making the other side do your parsing for you. Either way, the goal is that the message changes the receiver's state in one read, with no round trip.

The second rule is to send at all, and this is the one that actually kills teams.

A thin wire tempts you into batching. You will tell them later, at the call, in the summary, when you have the full picture. Meanwhile the other node is running on stale state. It is making decisions on what was true this morning, and the decisions are reasonable and wrong, and when the two of you finally sync, an afternoon of work has to be thrown away. The cost of an unsent message is not the message. It is every decision the other side made without it. On a thin wire, latency is more expensive than volume. A short message now beats a complete one tonight.

The third rule is that the wire carries more than facts, and this is the part I got wrong for years.

Two people on a hard problem are also each other's only reliable source of energy. Belief, doubt, exhaustion, the sense that this is worth doing. All of it has to cross the same wire, and it is the traffic people are most reluctant to send. A message saying I do not believe in this right now, or I am running on empty, or I need you to take this one because I cannot, feels like a failure to transmit. It is the most important packet on the link. The alternative is that one node quietly degrades while the other one, seeing only silence, assumes everything is fine. Then one day the wire goes dead and nobody saw it coming, because the one signal that would have warned them was the one signal neither side would send.

The asymmetry is the tell. If you look at who initiates, who follows up, who reports after a meeting without being asked, you can read the health of the link in the traffic pattern alone. A healthy wire has traffic in both directions and roughly equal in volume. A dying one has a single node doing all the sending and slowly concluding that it is alone.

Now the confession this essay owes you.

I am the noisy node. I preach compression and I send raw. When something is urgent to me I write long, unstructured messages at bad hours, with the conclusion buried somewhere in the second half and the reasons scattered around it, and I call the length urgency. I once wrote a letter to a colleague at three in the morning that ran to several thousand words, and if I had applied my own first rule to it, it would have been three hundred and landed harder. I knew this while I was writing it. I sent it anyway, because sending fast felt more important than sending well, and because being the one who sends a great deal lets you feel like the one holding the link together. It is not the same thing. A node that floods the wire is also a node that is hard to hear.

So the rules apply to the person writing them, in the order they most need them. Compress. Send now. Send the energy too, in both directions, and notice when only one side is transmitting.

Two people is the smallest team that can do something large and the only team where the wire is a single strand. Everything the two of you will build has to fit through it. It is worth knowing what it can carry, and treating every message as though the capacity were real, because it is.

Thoughts on goal-achieving

Imported from Medium. First published January 2024.

Motivation as a go-to tool

For most of my working life, motivation was my go-to mechanism for goal-achieving. You aim, plan, and then execute. If you don't feel like doing something, you push yourself. But eventually, I observed that motivation is not feasible for long-term goals.

In the past, I have been motivated to pursue some goals. I will be very motivated and full of energy when I start a project. But once I get over the obsession, I will start to procrastinate. I will try to push myself with some motivational literature, books, blogs, and videos. It will work for a short while. But next time, when procrastination kicks in, I will try to find something else to work on. My brain would come up with endless excuses not to do it. And in the end, I would simply avoid it. I have an endless list of projects I took on to work on, photography skills, exercises, meditation, but left incomplete.

Motivation is bursty. Motivation comes in spikes and goes. Motivation is not sustainable.

Having a system instead of conscious efforts

Taking from the book thinking fast and slow, we have two patterns in which we behave. One is with conscious efforts, when we can think rationally and are aware of our actions, each action requires effort. The second is autopilot mode, when our subconscious mind takes over and is effortless.

Having positive systems where you take actions without making conscious efforts is an optimal strategy. Advertisers nudge you towards certain purchases. “Opt for marketing” is generally enabled by default on most sign-up pages. Most do not make the effort to consciously uncheck the box or even notice it.

For me, going to the library is one such positive system. Once I go to the library, I usually stay for a productive session and cannot sleep. On the other hand, staying in a hostel room is a full-proof system to fall asleep. Staying in the room leads to sneaking into bed after a while, which then leads to sleeping or, even worse, doomscrolling. Opening Instagram reels or YouTube shorts is one such system that could easily capture someone for hours.

The power of habits and routines

Systems are created with habits. The book “Atomic Habit” is a great read and explains why habits shape most of our lives. Habit creation requires some conscious effort and is slow. It takes some weeks to start a new habit and for it to become a routine. Once a routine is created, an autonomous system is built that can work wonders towards a particular goal.

I used to struggle with starting my day early. I noticed that even if I used to wake up early, I used to sleep again. This cycle would lead me to wake up 4 to 5 hours later than a normal wake-up time. I started with the habit of brushing my teeth immediately after waking up. Once I brush my teeth, it becomes hard to fall asleep easily. This habit nudges me to work on something in the morning and leads to much more productive day.

On a microscale, the role of emotions

We have rational thinking, which could be overtaken by emotions.

Thinking rationally is hard when emotions are in charge. The brain plays tricks with random bullsh*t logic and tries to convince itself.

Some time back, I started to take cold showers. I read about the benefits, and I was convinced enough that it would help me stay disciplined, have focus for an extended period of time, and improve my metabolism in general. The first few days were great; there was a sense of excitement. However, one day, some thoughts started to pop up. “I could stay focused without the showers, too.” “Why hate me first thing in the morning?” “I slept late at night, so I could skip the day.”

My personal favorite rule to avoid such scenarios is the “5-minute rule.” When you are faced with something unpleasant, you set a goal to work for just 5 minutes. After that, you make a decision if you want to still avoid it. This rule works wonders for me. The initial delta to start something is the hardest part. Once I start to work, the rest is effortless. Our brain associates the initial pain with the entire duration, hence the resistance.

Motivation follows action, not the other way around.

With my limited exposure to religious texts, I find the way religions have survived interesting. I do not agree with some of the values religions advocate, but the way in which religions achieve these values is noteworthy. Religions require you to have faith in their teaching. Do your duties. Do not worry about the outcomes; just do what is required. Trust the process. Have faith.

Once you set the goal, do not question the process on the spur of your emotions. Show faith in the systems you created when you were rational. Take that leap of faith.

Exposure and numbing the emotions

I found that sometimes fear is one of the emotions that comes with achieving a goal. Fear could appear in the form of procrastination. I used to procrastinate on an aspect of my life. Taking psychotherapy led me to an interesting observation.

We sometimes procrastinate because of some trauma or fear associated with it. It could be due to very small actions, incidents, or behaviors in the past that have stayed inside you for a long time.

One of the ways to tackle fear is through continuous and incremental exposure. Continuous exposure helps to understand and look at fear from a rational perspective. This is the most helpful way I have found to tackle any fear. Nothing else will help more than taking action. Planning to do something is not doing it; thinking about something is not doing it; stressing over it is not doing it; it's the action that does the magic.
